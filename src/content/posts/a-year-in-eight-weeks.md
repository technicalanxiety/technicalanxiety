Not long ago I wrote about leaving. I ended that piece late at night, unemployed, calling it freedom, and I meant every word. The mine was behind me and I felt more alive than I had in years.

That essay had a clean ending. This one doesn't, because I've since found out what the clean ending left out.

I landed, and not just anywhere. It's a role I fought for, at a place people work hard to get into, on a team that has told me in eight different ways that they're glad I'm here. By every measure I use to judge these things, this is the best position of my career. I'm excelling at the thing I was brought in to do, in an environment that is healthy in ways the last one never was.

And the anxiety followed me.

The exit story has no room for this. When you close the work laptop for the last time and stand up from your desk at home, feeling free, you believe the weight lived inside that laptop, the gateway to a place you never want to return to, and that you're leaving it there. And yet, you are not. You carry it with you through the good interview and the hard negotiation and the first day at the better place, until one ordinary evening eight weeks in you notice it's still on your back, and you have to sit with a question you weren't expecting to ask this soon: if the environment is finally right, why is this still so heavy?

*I left the toxic place. I did not leave the weight. This is what nobody tells you about the good ending.*

---

There's an easy version of what I left, and it's a lie.

The easy version is that I escaped a toxic place. Toxic is the word everyone reaches for, and it isn't wrong, but it's lazy, and it lets me off a hook I don't deserve to be off. When most people say toxic they mean a place that grinds you down with demands, that takes and takes until there's nothing left. What happened to me was the opposite: a place that didn't take too much, because it couldn't take anything at all.

Up to that point I had been building something my entire career: skill, judgment, a track record, the kind of capital you spend to make things better. Then I landed somewhere that had no way to spend it. The value was real and the people around me could see it, but seeing it and being able to use it are different things, and none of them had the authority to cash it in. So it sat there, which is worse than it sounds, because capital that doesn't get spent stops earning, and capital that stops earning stops growing. I wasn't just failing to spend what I'd built. I stopped building, because there was no return to reinvest. Sure, I did the early work of learning a new place and found my footing fast, because that part is easy for me, but then I ran out of things that mattered.

That is a strange kind of starving, not too much work but too little that counted. My brain is a muscle and it was in a state of atrophy. I want to tell you that idleness is restful, but it is the furthest thing from it. Being overwhelmed at least means you're needed, while being idle in a place that can't use you means watching your own edge dull with nothing you can do about it. It was a Formula 1 car with an empty tank, an engine built for two hundred and forty miles an hour sitting still in the pit, burning nothing and going nowhere. Every day I sat in it telling myself the next lap would be the one they finally fueled it.

Here is the part I own: I stayed anyway, not for four months, but for years. Somewhere in those years I was transformed, and not for the better. It reached everything, my health and my habits included, and everyone around me noticed. I got complacent because complacency was easier than leaving, then I got contrary, with a belligerence that said I'm going to do this regardless of whether you want it or not, and the negativity started to feed itself. It was bad in every direction. I was, as I've put it to the people closest to me, screaming into my pillow.

My wife, who has been there through everything for my entire career, put it more precisely than I could. There was a reason I did what I did, she told me, and it wasn't ignorance. I'm very smart, she said, and I did see it. She said the smart part on purpose, because she knows nobody has ever doubted my experience and expertise more than I do. What I was actually doing was weighing what leaving would cost our family against what staying was costing me, and I kept choosing to carry more when I should have set that weight down and gone somewhere to pick up a new one.

That isn't something a toxic environment did to me. It's something I did inside an environment that gave me nothing to push against, and from the inside the two are easy to confuse.

*The place couldn't spend what I'd built. But I'm the one who stayed and let it decay. Naming that correctly is the whole reason the next part matters.*

---

The new place did not come easy, and the difficulty is the point.

Getting in was a long process, which is expected for a role like this at a place like this. What matters is what happened inside it. What they offered me first was not the role I have now. It was close, but close wasn't what I'd built a career to accept, so I turned it down and sat with the no for months. When I came back to it, I negotiated, not from leverage I was waving around, but from a quiet certainty that they needed what I carried and that I knew its worth, even if I never said so out loud. I moved the role to where it should have been and got what I came for.

And then, once I had it, someone said the thing that set the tone for everything after.

Now that we've met your expectations, the onus is on you to prove we made the right call.

I was unemployed when I heard that, and I want you to sit with the combination the way I've had to. You have fought for the elevated seat and won it, you're between paychecks, and the first thing waiting for you on the other side of the win is a bill: *prove it*, prove we were right. There is nothing unfair about that sentence. It's honest, and I'd probably say a version of it to someone I hired at that level, but honest and heavy are not opposites, and that one was both.

What I understand now, and couldn't feel in the moment, is that they gave me the fuel and I supplied the race car. The expectation they set was real, but the version of it that actually ran me for the next eight weeks was mine, built out of my own standards, which have always been higher for me than anyone else would think to set. They said prove it once. I said it to myself every day, before I'd finished my coffee and long after everyone else had logged off. No offer letter warns you about this, because the pressure they put on you is finite and the pressure you add to it is not.

*They gave me a sentence. I give them proof daily. Knowing which part came from them and which part came from me is the difference between a demand I can meet and one I'll chase forever.*

---

So I proved it, or I started to, which at this level never actually ends.

Let me describe the first eight weeks, and honestly, it feels like a year already. Every hour of every day went to something I had decided was worth doing, not busywork and not looking productive, but actual value, the kind you can point to. I learned the terrain, learned the people, learned where the real problems lived and started moving on them before anyone asked me to. I found my line and my pace, and then I kept pushing, because speed was the whole point and I had been starving for it.

I took corners I would have braked for anywhere else: hard problems, exposed positions, the kind of work where being wrong in front of people is a live possibility. I took them on because I could, and because a piece of me was still answering that sentence: *prove it*. My leader has a way of describing me that I won't forget. He says I'm the guy who looks at the hard thing and says hold my beer, and he's right. That is exactly who I am, and for eight weeks I was that guy every single day, into the evenings, past the point where the rest of the team had signed off.

None of that was them. The hours were mine, and so was the refusal to stop. They gave me a demanding role and honest expectations, and I took those and floored it, because that is what I do. I cannot sit still in a place that finally has fuel, because the alternative to going hard is going idle, and I already know exactly what idle does to me. I had just spent years watching idle rot me from the inside. Given a full tank, I was never going to drive it gently.

*Nobody made me do this. The place was healthy, and the pace was mine.*

---

I thought the weight was situational, that if I left the situation I'd drop the weight. That was the whole theory of the exit, and the theory was wrong.

Eight weeks in, doing the best work of my career among people I'd run through a wall for, and the anxiety is still there. It isn't the old anxiety, though. The old one was the dread of giving everything to a place that had no way to receive it. This one is the plain physical cost of carrying a real load at full speed for eight straight weeks, in an environment that finally makes the effort worth spending.

That distinction took me a while to see. In the old place I was exhausted by futility, and here I am exhausted by work. From the inside they feel the same, the same weight on the chest at the end of an evening where I don't have anything left. But they are not the same thing, and mistaking one for the other is how you talk yourself out of a good situation because it happens to be hard.

[I wrote before](/cost-of-being-the-canary/) about being the canary that survives the disaster and climbs right back into the cage. I'm back in the cage now, and that isn't changing. The difference is the mine, and I'm in a better one, with people who watch the canary instead of stepping over it, who notice when I go quiet and ask why. It's the same bird, with the same sensitivity that makes it useful and makes it tired. Completely different mine.

That is the thing I actually needed to understand, and the reason I'm writing this down before a year goes by and I forget I ever learned it. The weight was never going to lighten, and that was never an option. What changed is where it sits and who helps me lift it.

*I thought I was leaving the weight behind. I was only changing what kind of weight it was, and who stood next to me while I carried it.*

---

The best way I can explain the difference is a weight bench.

At a bench you choose the weight and load the bar yourself, so you're never handed more than you picked. The old place was that bench with a twist: I spent years searching for weight worth lifting, and every time I loaded the bar heavier, the room told me to rack it. Here there's no searching. There are tonnes of it, and the weight on the bar is whatever the work brings, whether I can lift it or not.

That would be a recipe for getting crushed if I were lifting alone. Every lifter has a weight and rep count where failure starts, and in a good gym the volume past that point gets carried by someone else. That's what a spotter is for. The first thing this place gave me was two of them, peer advisors with battle scars and more than enough years behind them to know what the first sessions feel like, and one is still the spotter I lean on. There's a process for it too, a way for anyone on the team to call for help when the bar starts coming down. Calling is our responsibility, and when we call, someone always responds.

The old place had heavy lifters too, and I want to be fair about that. Some of them were moving enormous weight. But everyone was lifting alone, rushing to stand out and be seen, so some of them got crushed in full view of a room too busy competing to notice. I was somewhere in between. The weight in both rooms was real, but the architecture was opposite: one room turned effort into visibility, and the other turns it into a call for help that someone answers.

My wife has told me the way I lift is normal for me, and she's right about that too: I go to failure until I find my max, then back off and work just under it. This time I didn't know where my max was. It had been years since anything asked me to find it, so the first eight weeks were failure sets, with my leader spotting me the whole way. When I hit failure, we both knew it, and now we're working just under it.

None of this means the place is perfect, and I'd be lying if I let it read that way. At any job there will always be something or someone that plain sucks, and even here, the work of lifting cleanly is part of the bad. The real question is whether everything else outweighs it, and I've come to judge that with four questions. Does the environment give me room to work through things the way that works for me? Does it constantly ask for feedback so it can improve? Does it actually implement suggestions? And when it rejects one, does it offer to collaborate?

I can answer yes to all four. In eight weeks I've made suggestions, and two were taken. Several others were rejected, and each came with a conversation about how to make what can't change right now a better experience. Where I came from, there was no way to make a suggestion and no feedback of any kind. Anywhere can say yes to a good idea, so the tell is what a place does when it says no, and whether it stays in the room afterward.

Eight weeks is a small sample, and it was collected while I'm still the new hire everyone is invested in. I hold it the way I'd want anyone to hold early data: I trust it, and I keep watching.

*The weight never lightened. What changed is that when the bar starts coming down, there are hands on it besides mine.*

---

I wrote everything above at eight weeks. I'm finishing it more than a month later, and the gap belongs in the piece. The draft sat untouched because the work was heavy and my writing time went with it, which is normal for me and nothing I'm going to apologize for. What the month gave me is something the first eight weeks couldn't: the chance to watch myself from slightly outside the thing I was describing. The recognition didn't come in one moment of clarity. It built up over weeks, a little at a time, while I was still lifting.

Some of the leveling off isn't mine to take credit for. The first eight weeks were the steepest stretch of the role, and the load has eased since. Most of what changed, though, is that I got stronger, which is the whole reason lifters train to failure. Failure isn't the goal. The muscle rebuilds heavier than it was, and the weight that crushed you in week three is the weight you warm up with in week ten.

Nobody stays at failure, though. You push to it for a stretch, then back off and train just under it, because a body that lives at failure tears. A torn muscle doesn't just stop you lifting. Within a few short weeks away from the bar you lose what you built, and I know exactly what that looks like, because it's the atrophy I spent years in, reached by a different road. So now I work just under my max on purpose, with my leader watching the same line I am.

There's a budget to all of this, and the gap in this draft shows exactly how I'm spending it. My hours are finite. Work gets what it needs, what's left goes to my family, and that leaves pennies for writing. For eight weeks I was borrowing against the credit I'd built with them, especially with my wife, and these last weeks I've been paying it back. Writing is what waited. At some point I'll re-budget, but for now this is the right spend.

The month also showed me which side of the bar I'm on. For eight weeks I was the one being spotted, and somewhere in the weeks since, that started to turn. I've been writing down how I work, in detail, for the people around me to pick up and use, not as a standard to follow but as a starting point to change. Every one of those documents says so at the top: change it, and send back what you changed. One of them tells the reader that if following it makes their work slower or worse, that's information about the guide, not about them. I wrote that line for whoever reads it on a bad day.

Nobody required any of it, and in the old place I'd have written the same documents and watched them go nowhere, because there was nothing there to receive them. Here the roll-out is slow and nobody has sent a change back yet, but there's a room to roll it out into, and that's the difference I couldn't see from inside the empty pit. An offering only stays an offering in a room built to receive it. Anywhere else it slowly becomes a sacrifice, and you don't notice the moment it turns.

*The weight never got lighter. It got shared, and now some of it is mine to help carry. Not resolution. Redistribution.*
